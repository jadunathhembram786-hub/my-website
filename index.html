Detailed Notes ✎

Detailed explanations written in exam-friendly language.

Plant-Water Relations

Basic idea: Water is fundamental to plant life. It functions as a solvent, reactant, transport medium and structural component, and its movement through the plant is governed by differences in water potential.

Why is water important to plants?

Water is one of the most abundant and physiologically important components of living plant cells. In actively growing tissues, water may constitute a very large proportion of the fresh weight. It provides the aqueous environment in which biochemical reactions take place and allows dissolved minerals, sugars and other substances to move from one region of the plant to another.

Water is also essential for maintaining cell turgidity. When water enters a plant cell, the vacuole expands and the protoplast presses against the cell wall, producing turgor pressure. This pressure helps maintain the shape of herbaceous tissues and provides mechanical support.

Physiological functions of water

  • Water acts as an important solvent for inorganic salts, sugars, gases and many other substances.
  • It participates directly in photosynthesis and several hydrolytic reactions.
  • It is involved in the transport of minerals from roots to shoots and the movement of organic materials through phloem.
  • Water maintains the hydration state of proteins, membranes and protoplasmic structures.
  • Evaporation of water during transpiration contributes to cooling of leaves.

Water Potential

Water potential is a measure of the tendency of water to move from one region to another. Pure water under standard reference conditions is assigned a water potential of zero. The presence of dissolved substances or pressure changes this value.

Ψw = Ψs + Ψp + Ψm

Where Ψw is water potential, Ψs is solute potential, Ψp is pressure potential and Ψm is matric potential. In many simple plant-water problems, matric potential is small and may be ignored.

Transpiration

Transpiration is the loss of water from the aerial parts of plants in the form of water vapour. Most transpiration occurs through stomata, although smaller amounts occur through the cuticle and lenticels.

The major physiological importance of transpiration is associated with the development of the transpiration pull, which contributes to the upward movement of water through the xylem.

Factors affecting transpiration

  • Light: Light generally promotes stomatal opening, thereby increasing transpiration.
  • Temperature: Higher temperature generally increases evaporation and may increase transpiration.
  • Humidity: High atmospheric humidity lowers the water-vapour gradient and usually reduces transpiration.
  • Wind: Moderate wind can remove humid air from the leaf surface and increase water loss.
  • Leaf characteristics: Cuticle thickness, stomatal number, leaf area and orientation strongly affect water loss.
